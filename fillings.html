Fillings

The bacteria in plaque produce acids that erode tooth enamel, eventually creating a hole, or cavity, in the tooth surface. Left untreated, this decay can spread to the interior of the tooth and may lead to the need for a crown, a root canal, or even extraction. When caught early, a filling can conservatively restore your tooth's function and appearance.

What kind of filling do you need?

Your choice of restoration depends on the location of the cavity and how much of the tooth structure is affected.

Direct filling. For most small to moderate cavities, a direct filling can be bonded to the tooth immediately after removing decay and cleaning and shaping the tooth. Composite resin, dental amalgam, and glass ionomer are typically used for this type of single-visit restoration.

Indirect filling. An indirect filling is often the right choice for molars and premolars with larger areas of decay or damage. Indirect fillings fit more securely, last longer than direct fillings, and can even strengthen the remaining tooth structure. An inlay fills the tooth surface inside the cusps, while an onlay typically covers the center of the tooth and one or more cusps. Indirect fillings can be made of composite resin, gold, or porcelain, and are created from a mold taken after the tooth is cleaned and prepped — the finished filling is bonded to the tooth at a second visit.

Filling options

Once you know what type of filling you need, you and your dentist can choose the best filling material for your tooth based on durability, aesthetics, longevity, and cost.

Composite resin. Made of acrylic resin mixed with powdered quartz, glass, or other ceramic particles, composite resin can be used for both direct and indirect fillings. It's popular, especially for front teeth, because it's color-matched to blend with natural enamel and generally preserves more of the tooth structure. It's more expensive than amalgam and, unlike porcelain, is more prone to staining over time.

Dental amalgam. An amalgam, or silver-colored filling, is composed of several metals, including silver, tin, copper, and mercury. Because it's very durable, it's often the filling of choice for biting surfaces and is one of the most cost-effective options. Amalgam won't blend with natural enamel and sometimes requires removing more tooth structure than composite resin.

Glass ionomer. This filling uses a flexible cement paste made with silicate glass powder that bonds to the tooth and hardens quickly — resin-modified versions harden even faster under a curing light. Many glass ionomer fillings release fluoride over time, helping prevent new cavities. They're less durable and don't blend with enamel as well as other tooth-colored options, so they're typically used for baby teeth, cavities along the gumline, or temporary fillings.

Gold. A gold filling is an alloy of gold, copper, and other metals. Gold is typically used in indirect fillings and is extremely durable, often lasting 20 years or more — a premium option reflected in its cost.

Porcelain. Made from specially formulated ceramic materials, porcelain fillings are used in indirect restorations. Because they're color-matched and translucent, they're the most natural-looking option, resistant to staining and cracking, and quite durable — with a cost comparable to gold.

If you have a small or moderate cavity, there are a variety of filling choices available to address your functional, aesthetic, and cost concerns. Discuss these options with Dr. Doshi to discover which filling is best suited to restore and protect your healthy, attractive smile.
