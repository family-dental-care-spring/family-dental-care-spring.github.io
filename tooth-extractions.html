Tooth Extractions

Dentists prefer to preserve your natural teeth as much as possible, but sometimes that isn't an option. There are two ways a tooth can be removed, depending on how severe the damage is:

  • Simple extraction. Local anesthesia is used — a numbing agent either applied or injected into the extraction site. Simple extractions are performed on teeth visible to the naked eye; an elevator and forceps are all that's needed to remove the tooth.
  • Surgical extraction. Performed under general anesthesia, surgical extractions are used when the tooth can't be easily accessed — either it hasn't fully erupted or it's broken under the gumline. A small incision is made in the gum so the surgeon can gain access; gum tissue is pushed back and surrounding bone (or part of the tooth itself) is removed, making it easier to remove the tooth with an elevator and forceps.

Tooth extractions are most commonly discussed in reference to removing wisdom teeth. However, an extraction can be required for a number of other reasons, such as tooth decay, gum disease, overcrowded teeth, impacted teeth, broken teeth, and baby teeth that haven't fallen out naturally.

Why you might need a tooth extraction

Tooth decay. This is the most common reason for removing a tooth. Patients who have avoided visiting their dentist for years may face this reality — without bi-yearly checkups and professional cleanings, early-onset decay can't be diagnosed before it's too late. Over time, a tooth will experience these stages:

  1. Tooth decay affects tooth enamel.
  2. Once worn through the enamel, the inner part of the tooth begins to deteriorate.
  3. As decay eats a hole through the center of the tooth, it suffers even more damage.
  4. When bacteria reach the center of the tooth, the result is a root canal infection.

The longer you go without treatment, the more severe the infection and damage will be. If your dentist determines your tooth can't be saved, an extraction — followed by a bridge or implant — will be strongly recommended. Cavities caught early can often be resolved with a simple filling instead.

Gum disease. Just as destructive as advanced tooth decay, gum disease attacks the gum tissue, ligaments, and bone that support the teeth. As these structures deteriorate, teeth become looser until they eventually fall out on their own or require extraction, followed by gum disease and tooth replacement treatment.

Overcrowded teeth. As part of an orthodontic treatment plan, an extraction may be necessary if there's overcrowding in the mouth — creating more space for the remaining teeth to be pulled and shifted into proper alignment.

Impacted teeth. Impaction occurs when a tooth hasn't fully erupted beyond the gumline, or only partially erupts. Overcrowding, a tooth that's twisted or tilted at an abnormal angle, or a tooth that comes in displaced are all reasons a tooth may become impacted — wisdom teeth are often impacted because the jaw isn't large enough to accommodate them.

Broken teeth. A tooth may need to be extracted when it breaks at or near the gumline, making it nearly impossible to perform a tooth restoration.

Baby teeth. Sometimes baby teeth come in at an abnormal position, causing the permanent tooth underneath it to not erupt normally. Removing this baby tooth can help guide the permanent tooth into its proper place.

If your dentist has recommended an extraction, ask about tooth replacement options — a bridge, implant, or denture — so your smile and bite stay complete.
