Root Canal Treatment

Every tooth has two sections: a crown, the visible part above the gumline, and one or more roots that anchor the tooth within the jaw. Each tooth has three basic layers — an outer layer of hard enamel (which protects the crown) or cementum (which covers the root), a middle layer of softer dentin, and at the center, the pulp.

Pulp is living tissue containing nerves, blood vessels, and connective tissue, found in the pulp chamber within the crown and in the root canals that lead from the chamber to the tip of each root. When pulp becomes infected or begins to die, it can't be restored — without prompt treatment, extraction is often the only option, and an infection can spread to the gum tissue, jawbone, and other parts of the body. Root canal treatment prevents tooth loss and further infection by removing the infected pulp and restoring the integrity of the tooth.

What can make root canal treatment necessary?

Even with the protective layers surrounding it, tooth pulp is vulnerable to infection and injury:

  • A deep cavity can extend to the pulp chamber, allowing bacteria to reach the pulp and cause infection.
  • A broken or cracked tooth can similarly allow bacteria access to the pulp chamber.
  • Serious gum disease can let infection spread from the gums to the pulp inside the roots.
  • Tooth trauma can critically damage the nerves or blood vessels inside the tooth, leading to pulp death.

The root canal procedure

A root canal is a relatively straightforward procedure. If you're anxious about it, your dentist can discuss sedation options with you.

  • First, the area around the tooth is numbed, and a dental dam is often placed to isolate the tooth.
  • An opening is made in the crown to access the pulp inside.
  • Small, precise instruments clean the inner pulp chamber and root canals, removing dead or dying tissue.
  • The area is thoroughly disinfected.
  • The inside of the tooth is shaped to hold a filling.
  • The roots and pulp chamber are filled with gutta-percha or a dental composite and sealed.

A crown is typically used to protect and reinforce the tooth after treatment. A temporary filling or crown prevents bacteria and food from entering the site while your permanent crown is created and placed at a later visit — the entire process usually takes one to three appointments.

Symptoms of pulp infection

A tooth that needs root canal treatment might be symptom-free, but any of the following are strong indications that the pulp has suffered infection or injury:

  • Persistent tooth pain, which can be dull or sharp and severe
  • Pain when chewing or when any pressure is placed on the tooth
  • Prolonged sensitivity to heat or cold
  • A cracked, broken, darkened, or discolored tooth
  • Gum tissue near the tooth that's sore, red, or swollen
  • A pimple-like bump on the gums that persists or keeps recurring — a sign of an abscess, an infection in the gum or bone near the root

A root canal should be performed by an endodontist or a dentist with special training in treating the interior of the tooth. Call our office right away if you notice any symptoms of pulp infection or injury — prompt treatment can relieve tooth pain and prevent further infection, and with proper care and regular checkups, your restored tooth can last a lifetime.
